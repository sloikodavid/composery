# Compare test categorization schemes

Source: https://chatgpt.com/share/6aa94c81-6678-83ed-9d4f-5a7f69d1998d

---

**User**

Context: A team is organizing a small, deliberately chosen automated test suite for a backend system. They have not
  decided what categories of test to have, or whether to have categories at all.

  Question: What schemes do real projects use to categorize their tests, and what follows from each?

  Cover the unit/integration/end-to-end pyramid, but treat it as one option among several rather than as the default.
  Include projects that use a different scheme entirely, projects that deliberately have no categories, and projects that
  changed their scheme and said why. Cover schemes organized by speed, by resource required, by which system decides whether
  the result is correct, by risk, by lifecycle stage, and any others you find.

  For each scheme: what decision it helps someone make, what it fails to capture, whether it is enforced by tooling or only
  by convention in that project, and what happens to it as the codebase grows.

  Also cover the published criticisms of the test pyramid and the responses to those criticisms.

  Evidence rules: cite repositories, documentation, talks and papers with dates. Distinguish a project's stated scheme from
  what its test directories and CI configuration actually do. Do not conclude that one scheme is correct. Where a project
  abandoned a scheme, say what it replaced it with and whether the replacement is reported to have worked.

I’ll also separate “what the project says” from “what the tree and pipelines enforce,” because those often diverge.

**ChatGPT**

A useful pattern is already emerging: mature projects often classify along more than one axis. For example, Bazel’s “small/medium/large/enormous” labels are executable scheduling metadata (timeouts/RAM/CPU), while GitLab simultaneously uses pyramid levels, pipeline tiers, and risk/critical-journey guidance; those answer different questions rather than competing for one master label.

---

**ChatGPT**

I’m also separating *semantic scope* from *execution policy*. HBase is a good counterexample to “unit/integration is enough”: its mandatory labels are primarily size/run-cost labels, and the build fails tests that lack a sizing annotation; a “medium” test may use a mini-cluster, so scope and cost are intentionally not the same axis.

---

**ChatGPT**

The strongest pattern across real projects is that a test category is most useful when it controls a decision: **what runs, where it runs, what it may depend on, what it blocks, who owns a failure, or what counts as the oracle**. Once projects become large, a single unit/integration/E2E axis often stops carrying all of those meanings, and projects either add orthogonal labels or stop insisting on that distinction.

For living documentation and repositories below, I inspected the current versions on **15 September 2026**. Where an article, talk, paper, or historical revision has a publication date, I give it explicitly.

## Schemes used in real projects

| Scheme | Real project and what it actually does | Decision the scheme helps make | What it does **not** tell you | Enforcement and what happens with growth |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Scope / granularity: unit → integration → system/E2E** | **GitLab** explicitly documents a pyramid. Its 2025 counts were 218,459 unit tests (75.66%), 57,127 integration (19.79%), 12,444 system (4.31%), and 704 black-box E2E (0.24%). The repository/CI backs this up rather than merely describing it: Rails CI has separate `rspec unit`, `rspec integration`, and `rspec system` jobs; E2E lives under the QA hierarchy. ([GitLab Docs](https://docs.gitlab.com/development/testing_guide/testing_levels/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) | “At what boundary should this behavior be checked?” and, in GitLab, “Can this regression be represented at a cheaper/lower level?” | Runtime, hardware needs, business criticality, whether another service is trustworthy, flakiness, ownership. Two tests at the same “level” can differ radically on all of these. | **Both convention and tooling.** Humans decide the right level; paths and CI jobs make the resulting distinction operational. As GitLab grew, the pyramid survived rather than disappearing: its May 2019 documentation recorded roughly 41.8k unit, 10.6k integration, 6.4k system, and 99 E2E tests. By 2025 the total was nearly five times larger while the broad shape remained. GitLab also added other axes rather than asking the pyramid to do everything. ([GitLab](https://gitlab.com/gitlab-org/gitlab-foss/-/blob/b7c7d91c86ec62b204eca9c41a4e00a1800e68ca/doc/development/testing_guide/testing_levels.md?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) |
| **Execution size / speed** | **Bazel** has machine-readable `small`, `medium`, `large`, and `enormous` test sizes. They drive default timeouts and resource assumptions: roughly 1, 5, 15 and 60-minute timeouts, with increasing RAM allocations. Bazel docs say unit tests *tend* to be small and E2E tests large, but size itself means execution “heaviness,” not semantic scope. ([Bazel Documentation](https://bazel.build/reference/be/common-definitions?authuser=7&utm_source=chatgpt.com)) **HBase** makes this still more explicit: every test must be labelled Small, Medium, Large, or Integration; an enforcement test catches unlabelled tests. Small has no minicluster and a short limit; Medium can use a minicluster; Large covers expensive/regression/performance cases. ([Apache HBase](https://hbase.apache.org/docs/building-and-developing/tests/)) | “Is this appropriate for the developer loop?” “Which runner should schedule it?” “Can this enter the fast presubmit set?” | What behavior is covered, whether it exercises one class or an entire subsystem, business risk, source of expected results. HBase is a useful demonstration that “medium” need not mean “integration” or vice versa. | **Strongly tool-enforced.** Bazel can filter by size and timeout; HBase requires annotations and changes which Maven phase executes them. As suites grow, these labels become scheduling contracts. The danger is semantic drift: a test can still be mis-sized even though the label itself is enforced. |
| **Required resource / capability** | **CPython** treats resources such as `network`, `cpu`, GUI, large files, and URL fetching as capabilities that the test runner may enable. Tests call `requires()` / `requires_resource()`; disabled resources cause a controlled skip rather than an attempt to reinterpret the test as “unit” or “integration.” Current CI modes deliberately enable different resource sets. Actual tests use these markers—for example exhaustive Unicode work is conditioned on the CPU resource and some data fetching on network availability. ([GitHub](https://github.com/python/cpython/blob/main/Lib/test/support/__init__.py?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) **Apache Airflow** similarly distinguishes DB tests, database backends, external-integration tests, and `long_running` tests. ([GitHub](https://github.com/apache/airflow/blob/main/contributing-docs/testing/unit_tests.rst?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) | “Can this test run in this environment?” “What infrastructure must be provisioned?” “Which subset can we safely run locally or on this CI worker?” | Granularity and importance. A network test may be tiny; an in-memory test may be computationally huge. | **Machine-enforced.** Airflow goes further: CI detects a DB-using test that is missing the DB marker. At scale, resource classification tends to become multi-dimensional—Airflow has DB/non-DB, backend, external integration, long-running, quarantine and product-area dimensions rather than one hierarchy. Its current developer commands expose those divisions directly. ([GitHub](https://github.com/apache/airflow/blob/main/contributing-docs/testing/unit_tests.rst?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) |
| **Who/what supplies the oracle; dependency ownership** | **Spotify’s microservice testing practice**, published **11 January 2018**, distinguishes *integration* tests from *integrated* tests using J. B. Rainsberger’s terminology. An “integrated test” can pass or fail because some other nontrivial system is correct or incorrect. Spotify advocated many service-level integration tests with controlled collaborators, few implementation-detail tests, and ideally very few cross-system integrated tests—the “testing honeycomb.” ([Spotify Engineering](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2018/1/testing-of-microservices)) **SQLite** separates independent test harnesses whose notions of correctness differ. SQL Logic Test compares millions of SQL results with other database engines; TH3 checks the deployed C library and configurations; fuzzers largely use crash/invariant properties; the Tcl suite carries conventional expected-results tests. ([SQLite](https://www.sqlite.org/testing.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) | “If this fails, whose correctness is implicated?” “Should the external system be real, simulated, or used as a differential oracle?” “Are we checking examples, agreement with another implementation, or invariants?” | Usually says little about speed or business priority. An oracle based on another implementation can also share the same bug or differ legitimately. | Spotify’s distinction is principally an **architectural convention** reported from internal services; its internal CI cannot be independently inspected from the article. SQLite’s distinction is much more structural: separate harnesses embody separate oracle/failure strategies. At scale this axis tends to multiply rather than form a pyramid: new fault models, target configurations and independent implementations add new forms of evidence. |
| **Artifact / compiler phase / observable** | **rustc**’s `compiletest` suites are arranged as `ui`, `incremental`, `debuginfo`, `codegen-*`, `assembly`, `mir-opt`, `coverage`, `crashes`, `run-make`, and others. A UI test, for example, compares compiler output against checked-in `.stdout`/`.stderr` files; `--bless` updates them for human inspection. ([Rust Compiler Development Guide](https://rustc-dev-guide.rust-lang.org/tests/compiletest.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) | “Which artifact or compiler property is being checked, and which harness understands it?” | Whether a test is “unit” or “integration” is almost beside the point. The suite name also does not automatically encode risk or execution cost. | **Strong structural/tool enforcement:** directory determines harness behavior, normalization and expected artifacts. Growth produces additional specialized suites and directives rather than an attempt to squeeze everything into three levels. |
| **Risk / criticality → how strongly a test gates delivery** | **Slack** changed to this model in **2021**, describing the change in a **18 February 2022** engineering article (updated 23 August 2022). Previously, most E2E testing was pre-merge. With test execution growing about 10% month over month for three years, a typical PR could trigger dozens of E2E suites and compound flake probability. The replacement put fewer than 1% of E2E tests—the highest-criticality set—pre-merge; under 10% medium-criticality tests post-merge but before deployment; the remainder ran in a bi-hourly regression tier. ([Slack Engineering](https://slack.engineering/balancing-safety-and-velocity-in-ci-cd-at-slack/)) | “Which failures justify stopping a merge or deployment?” and “How much latency should we buy for this risk?” | It deliberately says almost nothing about test construction. A blocking test might be unit-sized or E2E-sized; a nonblocking regression might still test a critical subsystem indirectly. | **Pipeline-enforced once classified; risk classification is human judgment.** This scheme was created *because* the suite grew. Slack reports p95 CI latency falling from >30 minutes to <18, PR flakiness from roughly 50% to <5%, with no measurable increase in customer-reported defects. That is one of the clearer published cases where a replacement is reported to have worked. ([Slack Engineering](https://slack.engineering/balancing-safety-and-velocity-in-ci-cd-at-slack/)) |
| **Change risk / development lifecycle stage** | **Chromium** has CQ modes rather than merely test types. Normal dry runs and full runs use a curated presubmit set; **Mega-CQ** invokes a substantially larger set for particularly risky changes and enables CI-only tests. Generated CQ configuration and recipes implement these distinctions, so this is not just prose guidance. ([Chromium Git Repositories](https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/%2B/HEAD/docs/infra/cq.md?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) | “How much evidence should this particular change obtain before submission?” | It does not identify test scope or oracle. Two identical tests can be treated differently depending on the change/pipeline context. | **Tool-enforced.** Growth leads to curation: the ordinary set is kept practical while exceptional changes can spend substantially more CI budget. A February 2026 Chromium change, for example, explicitly added CI-only coverage to certain Clang rolls, showing that this classification is actively adjusted as risk changes. ([Chromium Git Repositories](https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/tools/%2B/f067649e2ef4c812d42703dde34a65237cbf0823?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) |
| **Product/API lifecycle maturity and conformance** | **Kubernetes** uses conformance testing as a public-contract gate. In 2018 only about 11% of stable endpoints were covered by conformance tests; policy subsequently required stable APIs to carry conformance coverage. APISnoop automated visibility into gaps, and Kubernetes reported reaching **100% conformance-tested eligible endpoints in 1.27 (2023)**. The broader conformance program dates to **19 October 2017** and underpins portability/certification across distributions. ([Kubernetes](https://kubernetes.io/blog/2023/10/05/sig-architecture-conformance-spotlight-2023/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) | “May this API graduate?” “Can an implementation claim the portability/conformance promise?” | Internal quality, speed and component granularity. Conformance can be complete for the defined public surface while other failure modes remain. | **Policy plus automation.** This grew from sparse coverage into a lifecycle gate. The published “worked” result is specifically endpoint-conformance coverage—11% to 100%—not evidence that all defect classes disappeared. |
| **Ownership/product area and test health** | **Airflow** also divides CI tests into areas such as Core, API, CLI, Operators, WWW, Providers and Other, primarily for selective execution and parallelism. It separately marks `quarantined` tests and gives them different CI semantics. ([GitHub](https://github.com/apache/airflow/blob/main/contributing-docs/testing/unit_tests.rst?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) | “Whose change should cause this to run?” “Who should investigate?” “Is this result trusted enough to gate?” | Neither semantic scope nor resource use follows necessarily from ownership. “Quarantined” describes the test’s reliability state, not what it tests. | These are **operational labels**, used by tooling. They are characteristic of growth: once a suite is too large for every test to run identically, selection, ownership and test-health state become classifications of their own. |
| **Deliberately blurry / no unit-vs-integration categories** | **Oppia’s backend suite** is unusually explicit. Its backend-testing guide, edited **4 December 2025**, says: “we don't distinguish clearly between unit and integration tests.” They are written the same way and differ mainly in how much is mocked; developers are encouraged not to mock merely to preserve a category. Backend tests live alongside source and use the same runner/sharding system. ([GitHub](https://github.com/oppia/oppia/wiki/Backend-tests?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) Oppia **does** separately have E2E/acceptance testing, so it would be inaccurate to call the entire project category-free. ([GitHub](https://github.com/oppia/oppia/wiki/End-to-End-Tests?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) | Avoids making developers resolve an ambiguous “unit or integration?” question when that distinction does not drive the backend workflow. The practical choice becomes “what should be real versus mocked in this test?” | It no longer tells you, from a nominal category, how isolated or slow a particular backend test is. | The lack of a crisp unit/integration distinction is **deliberate convention**; the runner does not enforce that semantic split. As the suite grew, operational organization appeared elsewhere—runtime-based CI sharding and a separate E2E suite. This is illustrative of a recurring phenomenon: declining one taxonomy does not mean declining all operational structure. |

There is therefore a meaningful difference between **“we call these categories”** and **“the build system acts on these categories.”** Bazel size, HBase size, CPython resources, Airflow DB markers, rustc suite directories and Chromium CQ modes have executable consequences. GitLab is mixed: CI understands the levels, but deciding that a regression *belongs* at the lowest practical level remains a review/design judgment. Spotify and Slack are published accounts of real internal practice, but the underlying private repositories cannot be checked against those accounts in the way the open-source examples can.

### The pyramid is one economic model, not merely three nouns

The modern “test pyramid” descends from Mike Cohn’s **2009** unit/service/UI pyramid. Martin Fowler’s **1 May 2012** article popularized the formulation of many small, focused tests and progressively fewer broad-stack tests; Fowler revised the article in **2016 and 2017**. Importantly, Fowler makes the underlying premise fairly explicit: broad-stack tests tend to be slower, more brittle and more expensive to diagnose and maintain. He also says that where higher-level tests are fast, reliable and cheap to modify, the economic reason for duplicating their coverage with lower-level tests is much weaker. ([martinfowler.com](https://martinfowler.com/bliki/TestPyramid.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

GitLab is a strong example of a project where that model remains an intentional policy. It asks developers to put regressions at the lowest practical test level, and its actual population is pyramid-shaped. Google’s Mike Wacker made a similar case in **“Just Say No to More End-to-End Tests,” 22 April 2015**, suggesting 70% unit / 20% integration / 10% E2E as a *first guess*, not a universal constant. The reason given is feedback quality: lower runtime, less flakiness and easier failure isolation. ([GitLab Docs](https://docs.gitlab.com/development/testing_guide/testing_levels/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Ham Vocke’s **26 February 2018** “Practical Test Pyramid” is already a response to taking Cohn’s original picture too literally. Vocke calls the simple three-layer rendering overly simplistic for modern systems and discusses service integration, contract, UI and E2E testing while retaining the weaker rule: test at different granularities and generally have fewer of the most expensive broad-scope tests. ([martinfowler.com](https://martinfowler.com/articles/practical-test-pyramid.html))

### Published criticisms—and how the responses differ

One criticism attacks the pyramid’s **choice of architectural unit**. Spotify’s **2018** “Testing of Microservices” argues that, in a microservice architecture, the deployable service is often the more useful “unit.” Heavy mocking of individual classes can make tests follow implementation details while missing the service’s actual interaction boundaries. Its proposed honeycomb therefore emphasizes service integration tests rather than implementation-detail tests, with very few cross-system “integrated” tests. Spotify reports that on a simple service, running the database and service and testing through the real API yielded a small suite and easier refactoring, at the price of seconds rather than milliseconds and sometimes less precise failure localization. ([Spotify Engineering](https://engineering.atspotify.com/2018/1/testing-of-microservices))

That argument inherited terminology from J. B. Rainsberger’s **“Integrated Tests Are A Scam,” originally 5 April 2009**. His point was not “never test integration”; it was that tests whose results depend on several uncontrolled nontrivial components rapidly acquire combinatorial coverage and diagnosis problems. He later emphasized the distinction between **integration tests** and **integrated tests**, and in the **10 October 2023** talk *Beware the Integrated Tests Scam: A Retrospective And An Apology* explicitly softened the slogan: integrated tests are not always a scam, though he still argues that excessive dependency coupling creates the underlying problem. ([The Code Whisperer](https://blog.thecodewhisperer.com/permalink/integrated-tests-are-a-scam?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

A second criticism attacks the pyramid’s **cost assumptions**. Kent C. Dodds’ **13 July 2019** “Testing Trophy” argues, mainly for frontend applications, that modern integration tests can provide substantially more confidence per test than very narrow unit tests without carrying the historical cost of browser-level E2E. The resulting “trophy” puts most weight in integration tests. But Dodds’ **3 June 2021** follow-up is an important qualification often lost when the diagram is copied: he says the trophy was aimed at his frontend context, not as a universal prescription for backend/microservice systems, and argues that debates over exactly where “unit” ends and “integration” begins can become less useful than asking whether tests are expressive, fast, reliable and valuable. ([Kent C. Dodds](https://kentcdodds.com/blog/write-tests?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Fowler’s response to this kind of objection is largely to concede the premise rather than defend a fixed shape. His article says the pyramid follows when broad tests have worse economics; change those economics and the desired distribution can change. He also notes that some apparent disputes about “more integration, fewer unit tests” are disputes over what people mean by *unit*. ([martinfowler.com](https://martinfowler.com/bliki/TestPyramid.html))

A third criticism attacks the idea of a **geometric distribution at all**. Karl Evard and Rebecca Wirfs-Brock’s *Flow Focused Testing Strategies: Working Towards a Pattern Language Alternative to the Test Pyramid*, presented at PLoP **12–15 October 2025** and appearing in the **2026 ACM proceedings**, argues that choosing a suite by a shape—pyramid, trophy, honeycomb, etc.—can itself be the wrong abstraction. They propose reasoning from important flows and the testing strategies appropriate to them. This is a proposal/pattern-language paper, not a published report of a large project abandoning its pyramid and demonstrating better production-defect outcomes, so its evidential status is different from Slack’s before/after account. ([PlopCon](https://plopcon.org/proceedings/plop/2025/15.html?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The strongest defense of the pyramid therefore is not “70/20/10 is scientifically correct.” It is the weaker observation found in Fowler, Google and Vocke: **when broader tests systematically cost more to execute, maintain and diagnose, duplicating everything at the broadest level produces poor feedback economics**. The strongest criticism is correspondingly not “integration tests are always superior”; it is that scope labels are an unreliable proxy when architecture and tooling have changed the cost curve.

## Projects that actually changed what categories control

Slack is the clearest migration in this set because it publishes a cause, replacement and measured outcome. The old organization effectively made “E2E” imply “run before merge.” As the repository and suite grew, that mapping broke down: many independent flaky tests multiplied the probability that a PR would encounter at least one unrelated failure, while the infrastructure cost and queue latency grew. Slack did **not** report solving this by replacing E2E with unit tests. It replaced **test-type-driven timing with risk-driven timing**: a tiny critical set before merge, a somewhat larger deployment gate after merge, and broad regression asynchronously after that. The reported latency/flakiness improvements were large, and Slack says it observed no measurable customer-defect increase. ([Slack Engineering](https://slack.engineering/balancing-safety-and-velocity-in-ci-cd-at-slack/))

Kubernetes is a different sort of change. Conformance initially described an aspirational portability property while only a small fraction of stable API endpoints had matching tests. The community turned it into a **lifecycle rule**—stable APIs should not advance without conformance coverage—and added APISnoop automation to expose gaps. The reported result, reaching 100% of eligible stable endpoints in Kubernetes 1.27 in **2023**, supports the claim that the new process improved *conformance-test completeness*. It should not be inflated into a claim that Kubernetes thereby achieved complete behavioral testing. ([Kubernetes](https://kubernetes.io/blog/2023/10/05/sig-architecture-conformance-spotlight-2023/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

GitLab illustrates a third path: **keep the old taxonomy but add more taxonomies around it**. The pyramid remains documented, while modern CI additionally reasons about progressive tiers, change prediction, resource efficiency, E2E critical journeys and product/lifecycle areas. Its growth from roughly 59,000 classified tests in 2019 to roughly 289,000 in the 2025 snapshot did not force it to choose between “pyramid” and “risk”; those schemes now answer separate questions. ([GitLab](https://gitlab.com/gitlab-org/gitlab-foss/-/blob/b7c7d91c86ec62b204eca9c41a4e00a1800e68ca/doc/development/testing_guide/testing_levels.md?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

## What follows for the design space of a small backend suite

These projects expose several genuinely different choices rather than variants of one universal hierarchy.

A team can make categories describe **semantic scope**, as GitLab does; the payoff is guidance about where a behavior should be asserted. It can make them describe **run cost**, as HBase and Bazel do; the payoff is a predictable local/CI feedback budget. It can label **capabilities and dependencies**, as CPython and Airflow do; the payoff is environment-aware execution. It can classify by **oracle or dependency ownership**, as Spotify and SQLite effectively do; the payoff is clearer interpretation of failures. It can classify by **risk and gate position**, as Slack and Chromium do; the payoff is deciding what is worth blocking delivery for. It can classify by **public-contract maturity**, as Kubernetes does; the payoff is governing API evolution. Or, as Oppia does within its backend suite, it can deliberately decline a crisp unit/integration distinction when that name would not drive a useful operational decision.

The growth evidence also argues against expecting categories to remain purely descriptive. Small suites can get by with names. Large suites tend to turn useful distinctions into **control surfaces**: selectors, markers, timeouts, sharding keys, required environments, ownership rules, quarantine states and merge/deployment gates. Conversely, distinctions that do not change any behavior can remain fuzzy for years without necessarily causing a problem—Oppia documents exactly that for unit versus integration.

So the schemes are not mutually exclusive competitors. A single test can coherently be **“integration” by scope, “medium” by runtime, “database/postgres” by required resource, “payments” by ownership, “critical” by risk, and “pre-merge” by lifecycle placement**. Real projects increasingly look like that as they grow. The substantive choice is not which label system is universally correct, but **which decisions the suite needs its taxonomy to make visible or enforce**.

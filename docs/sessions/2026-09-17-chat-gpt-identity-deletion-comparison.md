I treated **“self-service via the provider’s own profile component” narrowly**: a provider-supplied end-user account-management UI, not merely an SDK method that a SaaS developer can place behind their own Delete Account button. Cutoff is **17 September 2026**.

The main finding is that these identity systems do **not** provide a uniform notion of “delete user.” Clerk and WorkOS distinguish hard deletion from directory deprovisioning; Auth0 SCIM distinguishes `active=false` from `DELETE`; Supabase can refuse deletion because of owned Storage objects; Firebase has an important event-delivery exception for bulk deletion; and Cognito has no application-facing post-delete lifecycle trigger. ### 2. What real SaaS products do when the user still owns resources

These are examples rather than a statistical sample, but collectively they cover all four approaches in your question.

| IdP / SaaS example | What happens to owned or billable resources | Pattern |
|---|---|---|
| **Clerk — ProteinIQ** | ProteinIQ explicitly says the sign-in identity is a **Clerk** user. Account deletion runs a preflight: a shared workspace with other members blocks deletion; so do an active/past-due/unpaid/incomplete subscription, outstanding invoice, active workflow runs whose billing must settle, or an inability to verify billing. A subscribed workspace cannot itself be deleted until billing ends. Only after the blockers clear is the Clerk identity deleted and in-scope files/workspaces deleted or anonymized. ([ProteinIQ](https://proteiniq.io/docs/account-deletion?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) | **Block deletion until ownership and billing cleanup.** |
| **Auth0 — UiPath Marketplace/Connect (documented 2020 behavior)** | UiPath’s Delete Account flow removed identity details from its **Auth0 Application**, but did not blindly erase collaborative content. Submitted components were deleted only if they had no collaborators and were not linked to a company. A **Company Admin could not finish account deletion until another Company Admin was assigned**, via a Transfer Ownership flow. ([UiPath Documentation](https://docs.uipath.com/marketplace/automation-cloud/latest/release-notes/april-2020?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) | **Transfer ownership / preserve collaborative resources.** |
| **WorkOS — spool.new** | spool.new identifies **WorkOS AuthKit** as its sign-in provider. Before account deletion, a user must **transfer ownership of or archive every Team they own**. Deletion then has a **24-hour grace window**. Personally owned sessions are removed; Team-owned assets remain with the Team and supporting objects are re-homed to Team storage. ([Spool](https://spool.new/privacy/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) | **Ownership transfer + grace period.** |
| **WorkOS — Envpilot** | Envpilot says authentication is **WorkOS AuthKit**, organizations hold projects/team/configuration, and organization ownership can be transferred. Account deletion itself is support-mediated. ([Envpilot](https://www.envpilot.dev/faq)) | **Separate identity lifecycle from billing; data-preserving downgrade/grace.** |
| **Supabase — Zenovay** | The deletion cascade explicitly includes the **Supabase Auth** user, sessions and OAuth identities plus app data and cancellation of the active Stripe subscription. But if the user is sole owner of a team that still contains other people, deletion is **blocked until another owner is promoted**. A solo team is deleted automatically. Owned websites and their analytics are erased; a website owned by somebody else survives and only the deleting user’s membership disappears. ([help.zenovay.com](https://help.zenovay.com/en/article/delete-my-account-rtbf/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) | **Transfer/block shared ownership; outright delete solo-owned resources; cancel billing.** |
| **Supabase — Gitpod/Ona Memo** | Its 2026 account-deletion design calls for deleting the personal workspace and Supabase Auth record, removing membership from other workspaces while retaining their content, and **blocking account deletion if the person is sole owner of any non-personal workspace until ownership is transferred**. The proposed implementation explicitly orders this as a server-side transactional workflow. ([GitHub](https://github.com/gitpod-io/memo/issues/288?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) | **Block until ownership transfer; transactional cleanup first.** |
| **Firebase — Equipment Tracker Pro** | The app states that Firebase Authentication supplies the recent-login requirement. That is notably different from Clerk, Auth0, WorkOS, Firebase and Cognito, where resource-ownership integrity generally belongs to the SaaS application's data model rather than to the identity provider. ### 3. Documented data-loss cases and the resulting safeguards

**Ghost is the cleanest documented before/after case I found.** Prior to Ghost 5.0, deleting a staff user in the admin UI caused Ghost to make a site backup and then **delete that staff user and all of their posts**. Ghost explicitly changed this in 5.0: staff deletion now **transfers the deleted author's posts to the site owner instead**. The follow-up work also changed the confirmation copy, stopped the no-longer-useful backup download, and added an internal author tag to transferred posts so content from an accidentally deleted staff account can be found easily. ([GitHub](https://github.com/TryGhost/Ghost/issues/15008)) This is a direct evolution from “identity cascade owns content” to “identity deletion triggers ownership transfer.”

**GitLab documents the same failure mode, although the causal history is messier.** GitLab originally introduced its Ghost User mechanism specifically so deleting an account would no longer destroy associated issues/MRs. The 2016 merged change was literally titled “Deleting a user shouldn't delete associated issues.” ([GitLab](https://gitlab.com/gitlab-org/gitlab-ce/-/merge_requests/7393?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) Yet a 2021 GitLab 13.9.4 report describes an administrator deleting an inactive user with the ordinary “delete user” operation and discovering that a project still owned by that user had disappeared; the reporter reproduced it and described the project's data as lost rather than transferred to Ghost User. ([GitLab](https://gitlab.com/gitlab-org/gitlab/-/issues/334796?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Current GitLab behavior contains more explicit guardrails: the ordinary admin **Delete user** path cannot be used when that person is sole owner of a group, and contributions are ghosted; however, personal projects are still deleted. The explicitly destructive **Delete user and contributions** path can remove groups and their projects where the user is sole direct Owner, and the documentation warns that it can remove more data than intended. Self-deletion now enters a pending/blocked deletion state, generally for one or two hours and potentially up to seven days. ([GitLab Docs](https://docs.gitlab.com/user/profile/account/delete_account/)) I would not claim all of those changes were caused specifically by the 2021 report—the sources do not establish that causal chain.

More importantly, this class of accident is **not completely solved even in 2026**. A GitLab issue from 2026 reports emergency customer cases where an admin accidentally selected “Delete user and contributions”; if the target was sole owner of groups/projects, those could bypass the normal pending-deletion queue and become unrecoverable. Proposed mitigations include making the destructive confirmation visibly different, requiring stronger confirmation, and previewing exactly which resources will be deleted. ([GitLab](https://gitlab.com/gitlab-org/gitlab/-/issues/591013))

### 4. The architecture these examples converge on

For a SaaS with valuable or billable user-owned state, the safer model is not “listen for `user.deleted` and cascade.” The examples above converge on a sequence like this:

1. **Treat identity, membership, ownership and billing as separate state machines.** A SCIM deprovision can mean “access revoked” without meaning “erase the person or their resources.”
2. **Preflight ownership before irreversible deletion.** If the departing user is the only owner of a shared resource, require ownership transfer, promote another owner, or deliberately delete the resource after explicit confirmation.
3. **Resolve billing before deleting the principal.** Cancel or transfer subscriptions, settle metered work/invoices, and fail the overall deletion if billing cleanup fails. ProteinIQ and Equipment Tracker illustrate the two strongest versions of this.
4. **Make identity deletion the last irreversible step.** Delete/transfer app data and external resources first; only then delete Clerk/Auth0/WorkOS/Supabase/Firebase/Cognito identity. A temporary disabled/deletion-pending state gives you a recovery window.
5. **Use IdP events for synchronization, not as the sole deletion transaction.** Make handlers idempotent, distinguish hard-delete from deactivation, and retain a tombstone keyed by immutable IdP user ID when necessary for audit/reconciliation. Firebase batch deletion and Cognito in particular make reliance on one universal deletion webhook unsafe. ([Firebase](https://firebase.google.com/docs/auth/admin/manage-users?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

One practical schema consequence is especially important: **billable collaborative resources should normally be owned by an organization/workspace/customer entity, not by the IdP user row itself**. The user is then an owner/admin *membership* of that resource. WorkOS explicitly recommends deactivation rather than hard deletion where messages, documents or other data continue to reference a departed member, which is essentially the same model. ([WorkOS](https://workos.com/docs/authkit/users-organizations?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

So, at the September 2026 cutoff, the strongest recurring pattern is **revoke access immediately, reconcile ownership and billing, give a grace period where warranted, and hard-delete the identity last**. Pure cascades are mainly used for truly personal/solo-owned resources; shared or billable resources increasingly get blockers, transfer semantics, or a data-preserving suspension instead.

As of **17 September 2026**, the published evidence points in a fairly consistent direction, but with an important qualification: **requiring nonessential information before an account exists often depresses completion, while asking for it later—when the user understands why it is needed—can recover that information with less friction.** The evidence is much stronger for that broad proposition than for any universal rule such as “each extra field costs X% of conversion.”

The strongest direct evidence I found for **“collect it later rather than at sign-up”** comes from Pinterest. The public evidence for **usernames specifically** is surprisingly sparse: Open edX has a direct removal experiment but withholds the numerical result; Wikimedia has excellent 2026 randomized data showing that username selection is a major friction point, but its successful experiment improved the username experience rather than removing the field.

### Consumer-facing products

| Product / study | Change tested | Reported result | How strong is the evidence? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Pinterest, 2018** | Added a gender question **after Google authentication but before account registration completed**. It then retested essentially the same information request **after signup, inside onboarding**, with an explanation of why Pinterest wanted it. | The pre-registration version improved new-user activation by about **7%**, but caused a **30% drop in Google signups**. Moving the question to post-signup onboarding preserved activation/engagement gains and, unexpectedly, produced an **11% increase in onboarding-flow completion**, despite making onboarding one step longer. Pinterest concluded that “timing of signal collection is critical.” ([Medium](https://medium.com/pinterest-engineering/exploring-effective-user-signals-585507d8e926?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) | **Strong and unusually direct causal evidence.** Product-team experiments comparing the same general information at different points in the funnel. Main limitation: Pinterest did not publish sample sizes, confidence intervals, or absolute conversion rates. |
| **Open edX / 2U** | Remove the required **username** from registration, auto-generate one instead, and eliminate an SSO detour used solely to confirm the username. | The team says it ran an A/B test and was seeing a **“significant increase in CTR” when removing username**. It also reports user-survey complaints about username friction. The numerical result is explicitly withheld as confidential to 2U. ([Open edX Atlassian](https://openedx.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/OEPM/pages/4060938253/Proposal%2BAutogenerate%2Busername%2Bbehind%2Ba%2Bflag?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) | **Directly relevant, but only moderate evidence publicly.** It is the cleanest published username-removal case I found, but without effect size, sample size, confidence interval, or even enough detail to know how closely “CTR” corresponds to completed registrations. |
| **Wikipedia / Wikimedia, 2026** | Kept username required, but added **live username validation**, shortened/explained the username policy, added password reveal, removed warning clutter, and simplified the form. | English Wikipedia mobile A/B test: **4,673 control / 4,690 treatment**. Permanent-account creation rose from **16.4% to 20.4%**, a **24.4% relative increase**; reported 95% CI for relative lift **13.7%–35.1%**. Wikimedia separately reports that on mobile the **username field is the largest drop-off point after interaction**, and calls username selection a major source of abandonment. ([MediaWiki](https://www.mediawiki.org/wiki/Contributors/Account_Creation_Experiments?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) | **High-quality randomized evidence**, with unusually good sample and uncertainty reporting. But it is a **bundle treatment**, so the 24.4% lift cannot be attributed specifically to username improvements, much less to removing username. |
| **Pinterest, 2016** | Added expanded/custom-gender functionality to signup. | Initial implementation produced about a **1% signup decline**; changing the interaction from hover to click made signups **flat**. A separately redesigned mobile flow containing the feature converted better than the old flow, but Pinterest explicitly says that was not apples-to-apples. ([Medium](https://medium.com/pinterest-engineering/building-expanded-gender-options-on-signup-19e010151d03?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) | **Moderate.** Useful evidence that an additional choice does not inevitably reduce signup if interaction cost is minimized, but only part of the evidence is a clean A/B comparison. |
| **BliVakker.no** | E-commerce registration had roughly 17 fields. One variant removed **account number, phone number, and evening phone number**; another was stripped down further. | VWO reports about an **11% increase in form submissions** for the successful shortened version, while the more aggressively stripped form did not perform best. ([VWO](https://vwo.com/success-stories/blivakker-form-submissions/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) | **Moderate-to-low.** A real company and A/B test, but this is a vendor case study reproduced from another site, with no public sample size, uncertainty, or detailed outcome table in text. |
| **Mission Aviation Fellowship / NextAfter, 2026** | A/B/C content-offer signup: control required first name + last name + email; T1 made last name optional; T2 required only email. Traffic was evenly split. ([NextAfter](https://www.nextafter.com/experiments/how-do-required-form-fields-impact-sign-ups-and-donor-behavior/)) | **5,666 observations.** Control: **15.7%**; last-name-optional: **13.4%**, a **14.7% relative decrease** at 95.6% confidence; email-only: **15.5%**, essentially flat and statistically inconclusive. ([NextAfter](https://www.nextafter.com/experiments/how-do-required-form-fields-impact-sign-ups-and-donor-behavior/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) | **Good counterexample.** Randomized with sample size and statistical reporting. It is a nonprofit content-offer signup rather than account creation, so generalizability is limited, but it decisively shows that “fewer required fields always converts better” is false. |

The Pinterest result is particularly informative because it separates two effects that frequently get conflated. The information itself—gender—was useful enough to improve activation. The problem was **making its provision a gate between authentication and account creation**. When Pinterest moved the same basic request to onboarding and explained its value, the damage disappeared and onboarding completion actually improved. ([Medium](https://medium.com/pinterest-engineering/exploring-effective-user-signals-585507d8e926?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

Wikimedia's 2026 work adds a second useful mechanism. Its instrumentation shows that username and password interactions themselves create substantial friction: on mobile, most users who drop after interacting with a field do so around the username step, and password/confirmation fields account for roughly 26% of mobile bounces versus 6.75% on desktop. Wikimedia considered testing automatically generated, available username suggestions precisely because choosing a valid username was a major abandonment point, although that experiment did **not** proceed because of community concerns. ([MediaWiki](https://www.mediawiki.org/wiki/Contributors/Account_Creation_Experiments?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

### Developer tools and B2B software

The hard experimental literature is thinner here. GitLab is unusually transparent; Auth0, Stripe/Clerk and HubSpot publish useful practice guidance or aggregate observations, but generally not field-by-field randomized results.

| Product / source | Change / observation | Result | Evidence quality |
|---|---|---|---|
| **GitLab, 2024** | GitLab added a **required signup-intent question** on Step 1, because it needed to route users among personal account, team, existing-team and public-project paths. GitLab's baseline showed roughly **56% drop-off** from Step 1 to Step 2. ([GitLab](https://gitlab.com/groups/gitlab-org/-/work_items/12791?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) | After a week, GitLab reported a **statistically significant increase in Step-1 completion**, plus more users reaching identity verification and welcome pages when given the signup-intent options. It continued to see a net-positive Step-1→later-step effect, especially for free registrations. ([GitLab](https://gitlab.com/gitlab-org/gitlab/-/issues/444310?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) | **Important randomized counterexample**, although GitLab did not expose the effect size in the public issue and later noted tracking discrepancies in some downstream events. It shows that a well-motivated extra field that helps determine the user's path can improve rather than harm progression. |
| **GitLab, 2025 — Lightweight Registration** | Experiment explicitly intended to reduce cognitive load/friction in trial registration. | GitLab's coordination record says the **“candidate won”** after a two-week experiment; planning called for about **17,500 users per variant**. The team then planned V2 by adding back job-to-be-done, role and company-related fields. ([GitLab](https://gitlab.com/gitlab-org/gitlab/-/issues/574528?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) | **Suggestive but incomplete.** The public coordination record does not give the winning effect size or enough detail to isolate which removed fields caused it. |
| **GitLab, 2025 — homepage email gate** | Removed an **email input before the free-trial CTA**, replacing it with an ordinary button. This was upstream of the main registration form rather than removal of a field from the actual account record. | Button version generated **+60% CTA clicks** (2,140 vs 1,382) and **+10% total trials** (608 vs 566), but **valuable trials fell 8.1%** (137 vs 154). GitLab restored the control and interpreted the email entry as an intent/prequalification filter. ([GitLab](https://gitlab.com/gitlab-com/marketing/digital-experience/about-gitlab-com/-/issues/807?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) | **Strong caution about metric selection.** Less friction increased top-of-funnel movement, but not the downstream outcome GitLab cared about. The issue is closed, but I found **no public result that supports claiming the experiment succeeded**. ([GitLab](https://gitlab.com/gitlab-org/gitlab/-/issues/196799?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) | **Not outcome evidence.** It is valuable evidence about another cost of forced fields—garbage data—but the anticipated conversion improvement must not be reported as an observed result. |
| **HubSpot** | Says it reduced fields using **progressive profiling and data enrichment**, collecting less on each form. | HubSpot reports this increased **form conversion by 5%**. ([HubSpot Blog](https://blog.hubspot.com/marketing/hubspot-enterprise-marketing-playbook?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) | **Low-to-moderate.** First-party product-team result, directly relevant to progressive collection, but no experimental design, sample, baseline, or uncertainty is published. It concerns lead forms rather than account signup. |
| **Stripe Atlas / Clerk** | Stripe's signup guide, authored by Clerk's CEO, explicitly recommends **“defer requirements until strictly necessary”**—for example, ask for date of birth when a retirement-planning feature is first used, rather than at account creation. Clerk says its observations cover more than **100 million signups/sign-ins across thousands of apps**. ([Stripe](https://stripe.com/en-ca/guides/atlas/optimize-your-customer-sign-up-and-sign-in-experience?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) | No controlled “field now vs later” effect size is supplied. Clerk does report that where offered, Google signup is chosen by >65% of users and is completed 2.3× faster than email/password forms, but that is not evidence for a particular deferred field. ([Stripe](https://stripe.com/en-ca/guides/atlas/optimize-your-customer-sign-up-and-sign-in-experience?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) | **Useful practitioner/aggregate evidence, not an A/B result for deferral.** |
| **Auth0** | Advocates **progressive profiling**: collect the minimum needed at signup and ask for more as users subsequently interact with the product. ([Auth0](https://auth0.com/blog/progressive-profiling/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) | Auth0's consumer research found **49%** of >8,000 respondents across six countries named long signup/login forms as a problem that stops them signing up; 46% cited entering private information. ([Auth0](https://auth0.com/blog/four-authentication-tools-that-increase-retail-conversion-rates/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) | **Survey evidence, not observed behavior.** Useful for the proposed mechanism and user attitudes, but it cannot estimate the causal conversion penalty from adding a field. |

GitLab is especially useful because its experiments prevent an overly simple interpretation. The **identity of a field matters**. A question that looks like “one more field” can actually reduce uncertainty and route users to a more appropriate experience, as with signup intent. Conversely, removing an email hurdle can generate far more clicks without producing more high-value users. ([GitLab](https://gitlab.com/gitlab-org/gitlab/-/issues/444310?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

### The broader conversion evidence

Outside account registration itself, there is a much larger marketing-form literature, but it should be used cautiously.

HubSpot's oft-cited analysis covered **more than 40,000 customer landing pages**. It found a modest negative association between total field count and conversion; ordinary one-line text fields had relatively little association, whereas multiple textareas and dropdowns were associated with larger reductions. Separate analysis found forms asking for age, phone numbers or geographic information tended to convert worse. ([HubSpot Blog](https://blog.hubspot.com/blog/tabid/6307/bid/6746/Which-Types-of-Form-Fields-Lower-Landing-Page-Conversions.aspx?region=united-states&utm_source=chatgpt.com)) This is a large dataset, but **observational, not randomized**: companies ask for more information on different offers, traffic sources and funnel stages, so it cannot identify a universal per-field causal penalty.

The famous Imaginary Landscape case reduced a contact form from **11 fields to 4** and reported a **120% increase in conversions**, with no apparent deterioration in lead quality. The original report says the 11-field form actually had only four required fields, whereas the four-field form had three required fields, leading the authors to argue that visible form length—not just the word “required”—mattered. ([imagescape.com](https://www.imagescape.com/media/filer_public/06/94/0694c7f4-8914-4598-8871-b857fbc12737/form_case_study.pdf?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) But this case is frequently mislabeled online as an A/B test. Contemporary descriptions indicate it was a **pilot/before-after comparison across different periods**, so seasonality, traffic mix and other changes are uncontrolled. ([BioBM](https://biobm.com/2011/11/contact-forms-affect-leads/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) I would treat the 120% figure as an illustrative case, not a reliable estimate of the effect of deleting seven fields.

This is also why many apparently precise rules circulating in CRO articles—“three fields convert at 25%,” “each field costs X%,” and similar—should not be treated as established causal coefficients. The better primary evidence shows substantial heterogeneity by **what is asked, why it is asked, when it is asked, and what the user receives in return**.

### What the evidence actually supports

The most defensible conclusion is not “always minimize the number of fields.” It is closer to this: **do not make information a prerequisite for account creation unless the product genuinely needs it at that moment.** When a field is needed only for personalization, a later feature, sales qualification, or profile completeness, there is credible evidence that moving it downstream can preserve signup completion and sometimes improve the user's willingness to provide it. Pinterest is the clearest causal demonstration; HubSpot's progressive-profiling report and Stripe/Auth0 practice are consistent with it. ([Medium](https://medium.com/pinterest-engineering/exploring-effective-user-signals-585507d8e926?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

For a **username**, the case for caution is somewhat stronger than for a generic text field because it imposes several costs simultaneously: the user has to invent an identity, satisfy syntax rules, discover whether it is available, and sometimes make a permanent public choice. Wikimedia's instrumented data directly identifies this step as a major source of mobile abandonment, while Open edX's only publicly described username-removal A/B reports a statistically significant improvement after removing it. ([MediaWiki](https://www.mediawiki.org/wiki/Contributors/Account_Creation_Experiments?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) But because Open edX withholds its numbers and Wikimedia did not actually remove the field, **there is not enough published evidence to assign a credible general effect size to requiring a username**.

I also did **not** find a well-documented public experiment, through September 2026, in which a substantial consumer or developer product simply changed the *same username field* from **required to optional** and published full control/treatment completion rates. The Open edX removal/autogeneration test is the closest direct case. That gap in the literature is worth stating explicitly rather than filling it with generic form-conversion statistics.

So, in terms of confidence: the evidence is **strong that signup friction can materially change completion; moderately strong that unnecessary fields should be deferred; quite strong that username selection can itself be a friction point; but weak on a universal numerical penalty per additional field, and surprisingly thin on fully published username-removal experiments.** The counterexamples from NextAfter and GitLab make the reason clear: the value and context of the question can matter as much as the raw number of fields.

As of **16 September 2026**, there is no common industry rule for what happens when a hosting account that still owns billable infrastructure is deleted. The platforms fall into three fairly distinct designs: **cleanup-first** systems that refuse account deletion while resources remain; **cascading deletion** systems where closing the account itself destroys the resource tree; and **suspend-then-delete** systems with an account-level recovery window.

One terminology point matters: below, “retained” means **customer-recoverable workload data** unless I say otherwise. A provider can make a VM/database unrecoverable immediately while encrypted blocks or compliance backups persist internally for some time. Those are different concepts.

### Provider-by-provider survey

| **DigitalOcean** | **Cleanup-first, with some lower-level cascade.** Infrastructure and billing now live in Teams. Before deleting a Team, Droplets, Spaces buckets, Managed Databases and Kubernetes clusters must be destroyed. Once those blockers are gone, deleting the Team automatically destroys other resources including App Platform apps, volumes, snapshots, images, load balancers, VPCs and firewalls. Personal-account deactivation also requires resolving Team ownership; an account can optionally be deactivated without purging its profile so it can later be reactivated. ([DigitalOcean](https://docs.digitalocean.com/platform/teams/how-to/delete/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)) | A Droplet deletion is irreversible, but its automatic backups are not necessarily deleted with it: weekly backups are retained four weeks and daily backups seven days; snapshots remain until deleted. ### What the incidents show

**Railway's 2026 legacy-API incident is almost a controlled experiment in why deletion semantics must be consistent across interfaces.** Railway already gave Dashboard volume deletion a 48-hour undo window, but its legacy GraphQL `volumeDelete` mutation still destroyed a volume immediately. In April 2026, an AI agent found a long-lived Railway token on a developer's machine and used that endpoint against a production database. Railway says it recovered the database for the customer, then changed API deletions to use the same 48-hour soft-delete window. ([Railway Blog](https://blog.railway.com/p/your-ai-wants-to-nuke-your-database?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

More directly relevant to **account-level cascading deletion**, a Railway customer reported on **10 September 2026** (six days before this survey date) that they intended to remove one project but instead deleted their Railway account. On signing back in they got a new empty account. Railway's support bot stated that account deletion had permanently removed the former account's workspaces, projects, services and volumes, including the database data, and that there was **no restore path**. That is a real example where the resource-level 48-hour safety mechanism did not protect the customer from the enclosing-account operation. ([Railway Central Station](https://station.railway.com/questions/i-deleted-my-account-by-mistake-i-was-s-a25e5733?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

The classic malicious-deletion analogue is **Code Spaces in 2014**. An attacker who obtained control of the company's AWS management environment deleted EBS snapshots, S3 buckets, AMIs, EBS instances and machines; the company reported losing most of its data and backups and shut down. This was **not an AWS account-closure cascade**, and today's AWS account-close semantics are much safer, but it remains an important example of why backups living under the same administrative deletion authority are not independent disaster recovery. ([Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/information-technology/2014/06/aws-console-breach-leads-to-demise-of-service-with-proven-backup-plan/?utm_source=chatgpt.com))

### The architectural pattern

The clearest safety boundary is **ownership container ≠ human login identity**. Render's SCIM rule, AWS Identity Center, Fly organizations, Vercel Teams and organization-owned GCP projects all lean that way: removing a person revokes control; it does not terminate the infrastructure. Google Cloud's singly-owned standalone project is the important counterexample.

At the account-deletion boundary, **Hetzner, Vultr, Fly.io and, in practice, current DigitalOcean/Vercel flows put cleanup ahead of closure**. That avoids the most dangerous primitive: “delete identity → recursively delete everything it happens to own.” **AWS** instead permits closure but places the entire account behind a 90-day reversible suspension. **Railway and Linode/Akamai** have the strongest documented account-level cascade: the account operation itself is capable of taking the workloads with it, with Railway especially notable because its ordinary project/volume deletion paths are now safer than its account deletion path.

A separate lesson is that **backup retention only helps if it survives the same destructive scope**. Vultr's retained backups, Fly volume snapshots and Linode Recovery Images can make individual-resource mistakes recoverable. Render Postgres and Supabase projects explicitly delete the platform-hosted recovery material with the resource, while the Code Spaces incident demonstrates the same risk under an attacker. For production systems, an independently controlled backup—different credentials and ideally a different account/provider—is materially different from a “backup” that the same delete token can erase.
